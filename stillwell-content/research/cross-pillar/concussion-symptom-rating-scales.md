# How concussion symptoms are measured: a plain-language guide to rating scales

> **Draft for founder review.** This is an educational resource, not medical advice. It doesn't diagnose concussion or tell you how severe yours is. If symptoms are getting worse, contact a clinician. For severe or rapidly worsening symptoms, seek emergency care now.

After a concussion, a clinician will often ask you to rate your symptoms: "How bad is your headache today, from none to severe?" That rating can seem small, but it is one of the main ways clinicians and researchers track recovery. This guide explains the most common rating scales, how they differ, and what a change in your numbers can and can't tell you.

## Why rate symptoms at all?

Concussion has no single blood test or scan that shows how recovery is going. Clinicians rely heavily on what you report. Rating symptoms the same way each time helps you:

- **See patterns.** For example, headaches that are worse on screen-heavy days.
- **Pace activity.** International guidance says it's fine to keep going if a symptom rises by **no more than 2 points on a 0–10 scale** compared with before the activity, and settles within an hour. A bigger or longer-lasting rise means stop and step back ([Amsterdam 2022 consensus](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/11/695)).
- **Have better conversations with a clinician.** A two-week record is more useful than "I think it's a bit better."

## The main scales, compared

| Scale | Developed by | What it asks | How it's rated | How it groups symptoms | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rivermead Post-Concussion Symptoms Questionnaire (RPQ)** | King et al., Rivermead Rehabilitation Centre, Oxford (1995) | 16 symptoms over the last 24 hours, **compared with before the injury** | 0–4 (not experienced → severe problem) | Somatic (body), emotional, cognitive. Also scored as RPQ-3 (headache, dizziness, nausea) and RPQ-13 | Adults; research and clinics; used in the Ontario adult guideline |
| **Post-Concussion Symptom Scale (PCSS)** | Lovell & Collins (sport concussion programmes) | 22 symptoms | 0–6 (none → severe) | Often grouped as physical, cognitive, emotional, sleep | Sport concussion and athletes |
| **SCAT6 symptom evaluation** | Concussion in Sport Group (Amsterdam 2022) | A symptom checklist inside a full sideline/clinic assessment | Graded severity | Not grouped; a total score and symptom count | Health professionals assessing athletes aged 13+ |
| **Neurobehavioral Symptom Inventory (NSI)** | Cicerone & Kalmar; used by the US military and VA | 22 symptoms and how much each has disturbed you since the injury | 0–4 (none, mild, moderate, severe, very severe) | Often grouped as vestibular, somatic, cognitive, affective | Service members and veterans; tracking over time ([health.mil](https://www.health.mil/Reference-Center/Publications/2020/07/31/Neurobehavioral-Symptom-Inventory-NSI)) |
| **0–10 numeric rating** | General clinical practice (not one owner) | One symptom at a time | 0 (none) to 10 (worst imaginable) | None, you choose the symptoms | Pacing and the "2-point rule" in Amsterdam 2022 |
| **PHQ-9 / GAD-7** | Spitzer, Williams & Kroenke | Low mood (9 items) and anxiety (7 items) over 2 weeks | 0–3 per item | Mood and anxiety only | Mood screening; recommended in persistent symptoms |

### Other ways clinics group symptoms

Clinics often sort symptoms into **profiles** to decide what kind of rehab may help. These groupings are made by clinicians after an exam, not from a questionnaire alone.

- **Pittsburgh (UPMC):** anxiety/mood, cognitive, migraine/headache, eye (ocular), balance (vestibular), with sleep and neck as add-ons. A newer trial adds exercise intolerance ([Elbin et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2023.1085662/pdf)).
- **Brain Trauma Foundation:** five subtypes (cognitive, eye, headache, balance, mood) plus two associated conditions (neck strain, sleep). In adults, the cognitive subtype was the most common ([BTF](https://braintrauma.org/concussion/guidelines/concussion-guidelines-step2-evidence-for-subtype-classification)).
- **Ontario guideline:** headache, sleep, mental health, thinking, balance and vision, and fatigue as its own area ([ONF](https://concussionsontario.org/sites/default/files/2023-03/Third_Edition.pdf)).

These groupings overlap a lot. Many people fit more than one, and that's normal.

## How to read a change in your scores

- **Small day-to-day changes are normal.** Sleep, stress and activity all move symptoms around. Look at the trend over a week or two, not single days.
- **Researchers define a "meaningful" change.** On the RPQ, a drop of about **4.6 points** in the total score is the smallest change people tend to notice as real ([Mercier et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2024.1482266/pdf)). These thresholds apply only to that exact questionnaire, scored its way.
- **Different scales can't be swapped.** A "3" on a 0–4 scale is not a "3" on a 0–6 or 0–10 scale. Stick with one scale so your history stays comparable.
- **"Compared with before the injury" matters.** Some people had headaches or poor sleep before their concussion. The RPQ asks about change from your usual self. Many scales simply ask "how much today."

## Questions to bring to your clinician

- Which scale would you like me to use, and how often?
- What change in my scores would make you want to see me sooner?
- Should I rate symptoms before and after activity, to use the 2-point rule?
- Which of my symptoms might point to something a quick exam can check (balance, eyes, neck)?

## When numbers don't matter: get help now

Don't wait for a trend if you have a headache that is getting worse, repeated vomiting, a seizure, increasing confusion or drowsiness, slurred speech, weakness or numbness, unequal pupils, or double vision. Seek emergency care.

**Draft recommendation for the app (to evaluate later):**
- Use Stillwell's **own symptom list**, written in our own words, on a **0–10 numeric rating** stored for every entry. This needs no licence and matches the 2-point rule.
- Let each person choose **how they enter it**: tap words (None / Mild / Moderate / Severe / Very severe, each fixed to a number) or tap a number 0–10. Both are taps, not sliders, and both save to the same 0–10 value, so history and the clinician view stay comparable.
- Don't let users switch between different published questionnaires for daily tracking. That breaks history.
- Offer validated questionnaires (PHQ-9/GAD-7 now; RPQ only if licensed) as optional weekly check-ins, clearly separate from the daily rating.
